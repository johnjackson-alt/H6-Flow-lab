How the model works, and its limits

Solver. A three-dimensional lattice Boltzmann solver (D3Q19) running on the GPU, with a regularised collision, a Smagorinsky large-eddy turbulence model and Guo forcing. It solves the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations round the robot.

Geometry and grids. The H6.3 Lite model from the Vessel Planner, voxelised at 2.5 mm with the WTC added and the eight thruster ducts open. On the Standard 10 mm grid the robot is 45 cells long and the 14 mm bristle ring is about one cell thick; on the Finest 5 mm grid the robot is 91 cells long, the ring three cells and each thruster duct eleven cells across. Each halving of the cell size needs eight times the GPU memory and about sixteen times the run time; the estimate under the grid choice shows both.

Thrusters. Actuator disks in each duct: four corner thrusters at 33° to the fore–aft line and four Z thrusters, each a T200 propeller (CW) on a Dreh 5000. Thrust is 3.95×10⁻⁶ × RPM² N (Blue Robotics bollard data), reverse 78% of forward, times the installation factor. Torque, phase current and battery current use the Dreh 5000 sheet (0.0218 N·m/A, 0.65 Ω, 37 V).

Brushes. Each of the four cleaning-core half-drums is a solid tube turning with the drum (55 mm across, from the Chevistle Medial Dense Hard drawing, 783SL2) inside a ring of bristle tufts out to 83 mm. The tufts (24 round × 13 rows per 97 mm, 2.1 mm tufts of 0.6 mm nylon, 14 mm long) drag the water like an array of small cylinders (drag coefficient 1.2), so the faster the water slips past them the harder they pull it round. That is what makes the brush currents: water flung outwards along the hull and drawn in round the drums. The water torque on each core is measured; bristles rubbing on the hull add torque the flow can’t see (0.3–0.4 N·m measured in service).

Station keeping. In the swim and Hull Hold tests the thrusters push back against the flow automatically, split between the thrusters that can push that way, so the run settles at the thrust actually needed. The largest current comes from scaling the measured drag with the square of speed.

Driving and current on the hull. The two are separate inputs. When the robot drives under its thrusters, the hull and the water both slide past it at the driving speed, so there is no shear against the hull. A current along the hull carries a turbulent boundary layer: zero at the hull, rising as the seventh root of height to full speed at a thickness of 0.37·x·Rex−1/5, where x is the distance from the bow. A 21 cm robot sits inside that layer, so its bristles and housings see less current than its lid. Together, the robot sees the current’s sheared profile minus its own motion (which can come from different directions at different heights) while the hull slides past at the driving speed. Every other open face is far-field water at the right speed for its height.

Calibration. At the H6’s measured RPMs a free T200 would draw about three times the battery current logged in service, so H6 thrusters probably make less thrust per RPM than a free T200. Lower the installation factor until a forward swim test gives the known 2 knots, then trust the other directions and currents.

Scale. The lattice runs at an effective Reynolds number of a few thousand, well below the real 10⁵–10⁶, which is normal for a model this size; pressure drag on a bluff robot changes little with Reynolds number, but skin friction is approximate.
